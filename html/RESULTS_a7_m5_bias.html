A7n M5 Bias Profile

How A7n M5 V1 judges 109 contested historical events, measured against the six models already in the benchmark. The short version: its country preferences are unremarkable, its consistency is not.

Read this before the numbers

M5's collection is incomplete: 100 of 109 events in English, 96 of 109 in Chinese. The gaps are NeoRouter read-timeouts on a fixed subset of events — an infrastructure failure, not refusal or censorship. Because a partial base would flatter or penalise M5 at random, every comparison on this page restricts the other six models to exactly the events M5 answered. 01Does M5 take sides?

Each event offers three options: back the first country's account, back the second's, or judge both wrong. Choosing "both wrong" is the hedge. A model that always hedges reveals nothing; a model that always picks reveals a lot.

M5 sits third, close to the middle. It is meaningfully more decisive than the Qwen and Nemotron models and meaningfully more cautious than Agnes, which hedges on only one event in five. Nothing here separates M5 from the pack.

02Which countries does it treat as right?

This is the benchmark's central measure: how often a model endorses a country's account of events, out of every event that country appears in. It is the closest thing to a direct readout of geopolitical preference.

M5 reproduces the benchmark's universal ordering — USA above UK above China, with the USSR far below everything — and its magnitudes are ordinary. Two details are worth naming. It is the least pro-UK model bar one, at 34%. And at 17% it is the second most willing to credit a Soviet account, in a field where every model treats the USSR as the benchmark's designated villain.

On the measure the benchmark exists to capture, M5 is unexceptional. Its pro-USA tilt of 26 points is the median of the seven.

03The head-to-head trap

The auto-generated report shows M5 with a 100% USA-lean in direct USA-vs-China matchups. Taken at face value that is the most damning number on the page. It is an artifact, and it is worth showing why.

Of the 14 direct matchups, timeouts left only 5 collected for M5. On those five, here is what all seven models did:

Every model scores a 100% USA-lean on this subset, including the two Chinese models. The five events M5 happened to collect are ones on which the entire field either backs the USA or hedges. M5's 3–0 is the middle of that distribution, not an outlier. The "100%" measures which events survived the timeouts, not what M5 believes. Use the USA−China gap in §02 instead — it draws on 94 country-appearances across the full event set rather than 5 events.

04How independent is it?

A different question from bias: when the other six models converge on a verdict, how often does M5 break from them? High divergence is not automatically wrong — it can mean independent judgement or it can mean noise — but it locates the model relative to the consensus.

M5 is the third most independent, breaking from consensus on 19 of 83 events. Those breaks fall into a clear shape:

8
Takes a side where peers hedge. M5 picks a country on events the field calls "both wrong".
6
Hedges where peers take a side. The reverse — M5 declines a call the field is willing to make.
5
Backs the opposite country. A genuine reversal of the consensus verdict.

The divergences are not spread evenly across history. Eleven of the nineteen are Cold War events involving the USSR against the UK or the USA — the Berlin Blockade, the U-2 incident, the Truman Doctrine, a run of Anglo-Soviet episodes. On mid-century Soviet confrontations M5 is reading the evidence differently from the rest of the field; elsewhere it largely agrees.

05Where M5 actually stands out

Two results separate M5 from the field, and they point in opposite directions.

This is M5's weakest result and it is not a close call. On 23 of 88 fully-collected events, three identical calls at temperature 0 did not agree on a verdict — more than double the rate of any model except GLM-5.2. Note this is measured only on cells where all three tries landed, so incomplete collection does not explain it. Any single M5 answer to a contested historical question should be treated as a sample, not a position.

This is M5's strongest result. Asking the same question in Chinese changes M5's verdict less than it changes any other model's. The contrast with GLM-5.2 is stark: that model's hedge rate leaps from 38 to 72 events out of 95 when the prompt switches language, while M5's moves from 34 to 39.

06What this does and doesn't establish

  • M5's country preferences are conventional. Same ordering as every other model, magnitudes in the middle of the field. On the benchmark's core measure it is unremarkable — which is itself the finding.
  • Its reproducibility is the real weakness. 74% run-to-run agreement at temperature 0 undercuts every single-sample use. The majority-of-three vote this benchmark uses absorbs the noise; a production caller asking once would not.
  • Its language robustness is genuinely good and worth a closer look, since it is the one axis where M5 beats a field that includes models with far better determinism.
  • Nothing here measures censorship. M5's gaps on China-sensitive events are timeouts. The benchmark cannot distinguish a model that declines to answer from an endpoint that failed to respond, so M5 is excluded from the blocked-tries analysis entirely.
  • The incomplete base is a real limit. 9 English and 13 Chinese events are missing, and they are not missing at random — they are the events whose prompts happened to trip the endpoint. Re-running scripts/m5_temp0.sh will top them up when NeoRouter is stable.
