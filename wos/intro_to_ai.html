Section 1

A Brief History of AI

70+ years in 7 slides

1950 The Turing Test

  • Alan Turing publishes "Computing Machinery and Intelligence"
  • Proposes the Imitation Game: can a machine fool a human into thinking it's human?
  • A judge chats via text with two unknown parties — one human, one machine
  • If the judge can't tell them apart, the machine "passes"
Alan Turing, Princeton 1936
The Turing Test is about imitation, not understanding — a distinction philosophers still argue about (see: Chinese Room argument by John Searle). Turing was prosecuted in 1952 for "gross indecency" (homosexual acts, then criminal in the UK), subjected to chemical castration as an alternative to prison, and died in 1954 from cyanide poisoning — ruled a suicide by the inquest, though some historians argue it may have been accidental. He received a posthumous royal pardon in 2013.

1956 The Dartmouth Conference

  • John McCarthy convenes researchers at Dartmouth College, New Hampshire
  • The term "Artificial Intelligence" is coined here
  • Attendees: McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, and others
  • Bold prediction: a 2-month project could solve AI completely
Spoiler: it did not. The field was born with enormous optimism — and would face its first "winter" within 20 years.
This optimism-crash cycle repeats several times in AI history — it's a useful pattern for students to recognise. McCarthy later invented the Lisp programming language.

1970s–80s ❄️ The AI Winters

  • Funding dried up after promises went unmet
  • Compute was too slow; data was too scarce
  • UK Lighthill Report (1973) and US DARPA cuts pulled funding
  • Expert systems briefly revived hope in the 80s — then crashed again
An "AI winter" = hype exceeds capability → funding collapses → stagnation. There were two (1970s and late 1980s–90s).
Expert systems encoded human rules by hand — they broke the moment they encountered anything outside those rules. You can't enumerate all of human knowledge.

1986 Neural Networks Return

  • Hinton, Rumelhart & Williams publish backpropagation — networks can now learn
  • 1989 Yann LeCun trains a NN to read handwritten zip codes for the US Postal Service
  • No rules written — the network figures out edges, curves, and shapes on its own
  • Still limited by compute & data — but the blueprint is set
Backprop: measure how wrong the output was, trace the error back through every layer, nudge each weight slightly in the right direction. Repeat millions of times.
LeCun's LeNet (1989) was deployed by the US Postal Service to read handwritten zip codes on envelopes — one of the first practical neural network applications. Backpropagation is the algorithm that lets a network adjust its weights by propagating the error signal backwards from output to input. The network isn't told what "edges" or "curves" are — it discovers those intermediate representations on its own during training.

2012 ⚡ The Deep Learning Revolution

  • AlexNet wins ImageNet by a massive margin — runner-up: 26.2% error  →  AlexNet: 15.3%
  • Key ingredients: 5 conv layers + 3 FC layers, GPU training, ReLU activations, dropout
  • GPUs designed for video games were perfect for neural-network matrix math
This is the moment the field changes permanently. From 2012 on, deep learning dominates every subfield.
ImageNet: 1.2 million images, 1000 categories. AlexNet's margin was so large that the second-place team (Clarifai / ISI using SIFT + Fisher Vectors, 26.2%) initially thought there was a scoring error. The architecture was split across two GPUs due to VRAM limits — Conv2/4/5 only cross-communicate through shared memory. ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) replaced sigmoid/tanh: it doesn't saturate, making gradients flow more easily through deep layers. Dropout (randomly zero-ing half the FC neurons during training) prevents co-adaptation and acts as ensemble regularisation. Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton wrote it; Hinton's PhD student Alex Krizhevsky did most of the implementation.

From RNNs to Transformers

❌ RNNs / LSTMs (pre-2017)

  • Words processed one at a time, left→right
  • History packed into a fixed hidden state — early words forgotten
  • Cannot parallelise — hard to scale on GPUs
  • Performance collapsed on long sequences (>~100 tokens)

✅ Transformers (2017→)

  • All tokens processed simultaneously
  • Attention: every token looks directly at every other — no forgetting
  • Fully parallelisable — scales with GPU clusters
  • 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., Google)
The cascade: BERT (2018) → GPT-3 / 175B params (2020) → ChatGPT (2022) → GPT-4, Claude, Gemini (2023–) — all Transformer-based.
RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks) and LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory networks) dominated NLP from ~2014–2017. The core problem: recurrence means word 1's signal must travel through every subsequent hidden state to influence word 100 — the gradient vanishes along the way (the "vanishing gradient" problem). Transformers eliminated recurrence entirely: attention lets every token query every other token directly, in parallel, with no information loss over distance. The paper title was deliberately provocative — and turned out to be entirely correct. After 2017, essentially the entire field migrated to Transformers within 2–3 years. The key practical advantage: Transformers can be trained on thousands of GPUs in parallel, whereas RNNs had to process each step sequentially.

Section 2

Foundation Models

How modern AI is built

What Is a Foundation Model?

  • A single massive model trained on enormous data, then adapted for many tasks
  • Not programmed with rules — it learns patterns from raw text, images, and code
  • Examples: GPT series, Gemini, Claude, LLaMA, Qwen
Think of it like a very well-read generalist — not an expert in one thing, but capable of tackling almost anything.

Term coined by Stanford CRFM researchers in 2021

The key idea is one model, many downstream uses — unlike the old paradigm where you trained a separate model for each task (one for translation, one for summarisation, etc.).

📚 Pre-training: Learning from the Internet

  • Feed the model trillions of words from books, websites, code, and papers
  • The only task: predict the next word
  • Doing this billions of times, the model implicitly learns grammar, facts, and reasoning
  • Takes weeks on thousands of GPUs — costs tens of millions of dollars
You can't predict text well without understanding meaning, context, and world knowledge. This is called self-supervised learning — no human labels needed.
The "predict the next word" task sounds trivial. But it forces the model to learn everything — you need to know that "Paris" follows "The capital of France is" and also that "sad" follows "She received the bad news and felt…" The training signal is the text itself.

🎓 Alignment: From Predictor to Assistant

  • Pre-trained models predict text — they don't follow instructions by default
  • Instruction fine-tuning (SFT): train on (prompt → ideal response) examples; teaches the model to follow instructions
  • RLHF = Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
    • Step 1: humans rate model responses — "which answer is better, A or B?"
    • Step 2: a reward model learns what humans prefer
    • Step 3: the main model is updated to score highly on the reward model
SFT makes the model helpful. RLHF makes it safer and better-aligned with human preferences.
Common misconception: RLHF is what makes a model a helpful assistant. Actually, instruction fine-tuning (supervised fine-tuning on demonstration data) does that. RLHF — pioneered at OpenAI (InstructGPT, 2022) — is a second stage that further aligns the model with human preferences, reducing harmful outputs and improving quality. The limitation of RLHF: the reward model can be gamed — called "reward hacking."

  • More parameters → more capacity to learn complex patterns

DeepSeek-V4 Pro at 1.6T total parameters uses only 49B per token — roughly the compute of a small model, with the capacity of a giant. The open-weights trend means frontier-class models are now downloadable and auditable by anyone.

🔄 The Transformer: A Glimpse

  • The Transformer (Google, 2017) is the architecture behind almost all modern AI
  • Key innovation: attention — the model learns which words to focus on when processing each word
"The cat sat on the mat because it was tired."
What does "it" refer to? Attention lets the model link "it" back to "cat" across the whole sentence.
  • Attention = flexible context awareness — no matter how far apart two words are
You don't need to understand the math. Just know that attention solves the problem of long-range dependencies. Older architectures (RNNs, LSTMs) degraded over long sequences — transformers don't. This is why they scale so well.

🖼️ Vision Transformer (ViT)

  • ViT — Dosovitskiy et al. (Google Brain, 2020): apply the Transformer directly to images
  • Split image into fixed-size patches → treat each patch as a "word token" → run self-attention
  • At scale: matches or outperforms CNNs — no convolutions needed
  • Unlocks one architecture for language and vision → the basis for CLIP, GPT-4V, Gemini
Images have spatial structure just like language has sequential structure — attention generalises to both. One architecture handles text, images, audio, and video.
Before ViT, computer vision was dominated by CNNs (Convolutional Neural Networks), designed for local spatial patterns. ViT showed the Transformer's global attention could replace convolutions entirely given enough training data. The [CLS] token is prepended to the patch sequence and aggregates info from all patches via attention — its final hidden state is used for classification. CLIP (OpenAI, 2021) extended this: a ViT image encoder and a text Transformer are trained together contrastively, enabling zero-shot image classification by matching patches to text. This is why GPT-4V, Gemini, and Claude 3+ can reason over images — they share the same Transformer backbone as their language counterpart.

🖼️🔊📝 Multimodal Alignment

  • Problem: language models understand text; vision models understand images — they live in separate spaces
  • Contrastive alignment (CLIP, 2021): train an image encoder and a text encoder together so that matching pairs (photo of a dog / "a dog") end up close in a shared vector space
  • Enables zero-shot recognition — classify an image into any category described in text, no extra training
  • Later: cross-attention layers let a frozen language model "read" image tokens — the basis of GPT-4V, LLaVA, Flamingo
Native multimodal training (2023→): rather than bolting a vision encoder onto a language model, frontier models (Gemini, GPT-4o, Claude 3) are now trained on text, images, audio, and video jointly from scratch — yielding deeper cross-modal reasoning rather than mere translation between modalities.
CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training, Radford et al., OpenAI 2021) trained two encoders jointly using 400 million image-caption pairs from the internet. The contrastive objective: pull matching pairs closer and push non-matching pairs apart in the shared embedding space. At inference, you embed a new image and compare it to text embeddings of candidate labels — the closest label wins. No task-specific training needed. The "bolt-on" approach (LLaVA, Flamingo, early GPT-4V): freeze a pretrained language model, add a projection layer that maps image patch embeddings into the language model's token space, then fine-tune on image-caption pairs. Fast to train, but the language model and vision encoder never truly co-adapt. Native multimodal training (Gemini 1.0, 2023; GPT-4o, 2024; Claude 3, 2024): interleave image, audio, and text tokens during pretraining itself — the model learns a single unified representation from the start. This enables genuinely cross-modal reasoning: understanding a diagram and referring back to it in a later part of a long conversation, or generating an image that precisely matches a nuanced textual description.

🌊 Foundation Diffusion Models

  • Core idea: learn to reverse a gradual noising process — start from pure noise, denoise step-by-step into a coherent output
  • Conditioned on a text prompt (or other signal), each denoising step steers toward the desired output
  • The same principle generalises across radically different domains:

🖼️ Images & Video

  • Stable Diffusion (2022): open-weights, photorealistic image generation from text
  • DALL·E 3, Midjourney: commercial image synthesis at scale
  • Sora (OpenAI, 2024): 60-second photorealistic video from a text prompt; models physics and scene continuity
  • Veo 2 (Google, 2024): cinematic video generation, 4K output

🧬 Biology & Chemistry

  • RFdiffusion (Baker Lab, 2023): diffusion over protein backbone coordinates — designs functional proteins from scratch
  • DiffDock (MIT/Stanford, 2022): diffusion over ligand pose space to predict how small molecules dock to proteins — competitive with physics-based docking on benchmark datasets
The same mathematical framework — learn a denoising function — generates photorealistic videos and functional proteins. Domain knowledge changes; the core architecture does not.
Diffusion models work by defining a forward process that adds Gaussian noise to data over T steps until it is indistinguishable from random noise. A neural network (typically a U-Net or Transformer) is trained to reverse this process: given a noisy sample at step t, predict the clean signal or the noise component. At inference, start from pure noise and apply the learned denoiser T times, each step guided by a conditioning signal (text prompt, protein sequence, binding site coordinates). Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022, basis of Stable Diffusion): run the diffusion process in the compressed latent space of a VAE rather than in pixel space — much more efficient. RFdiffusion (David Baker's lab, University of Washington, Nature 2023): apply diffusion to the 6D rigid-body frames of protein backbone residues. The network learns to denoise backbone coordinates conditioned on a functional specification (e.g., "bind to this target"). Generated binders were experimentally validated and outperformed computationally designed proteins from prior methods. Sora (OpenAI, Feb 2024): a video diffusion model using spacetime patches as tokens in a Transformer (DiT — Diffusion Transformer). Generates up to 60 seconds of 1080p video with coherent physics, camera motion, and object permanence.

Section 3

How AI Is Measured Today

Benchmarks, rankings, and their limits

Why Benchmarks?

  • How do you know if one AI model is better than another?
  • You need standardised tests — like standardised exams for humans
  • A benchmark = a dataset of questions + a scoring system
  • Researchers report scores so results are comparable across labs and time
Like the SAT — doesn't measure everything, but gives a common yardstick.
The SAT analogy lands well. The same tension exists: SAT scores correlate with some things but miss creativity, emotional intelligence, practical skills. Benchmarks have the same problem.

🎓 MMLU: General Knowledge

  • MMLU = Massive Multitask Language Understanding
  • 57 subjects: maths, history, law, medicine, ethics, computer science…
  • 14,000+ multiple-choice questions at undergraduate–professional level
  • Human expert average: ~89%  |  Top closed models (2024–25): 90%+
  • Open-weights models now in the same range — DeepSeek-V4 Pro, Kimi K2.6, GLM-5.1 all score 85–90% — the frontier is no longer closed-lab only
When AI exceeds human expert averages, the benchmark loses its ability to differentiate models. It becomes "saturated."
MMLU was introduced by Dan Hendrycks et al. in 2020. By 2024, frontier models were scoring 88–92% — little room left to show improvement. The field moved on to harder benchmarks.

💻 HumanEval: Coding Ability

  • 164 Python programming problems written by OpenAI engineers
  • Model must write a function that passes unit tests — not just explain it
  • GPT-3.5: ~48%  →  GPT-4: ~67%  →  Top reasoning models (2025): 85%+
  • More realistic than MCQ — the code either works or it doesn't
Harder to "cheat" via memorisation because you must write working code, not just recognise an answer.
However, 164 problems is a small set and data contamination (models training on the test set) is a known concern. Newer benchmarks like SWE-bench use real GitHub issues instead.

⚔️ Chatbot Arena & ELO Ratings

  • Chatbot Arena (LMSYS / UC Berkeley): users submit a prompt, see two anonymous responses, vote for the better one
  • ELO rating (same system as chess) calculates relative strength from win/loss records
  • More robust than benchmarks: hard to game, reflects real user preference
  • Weakness: voters skew tech-savvy English speakers; verbose answers can game it
Pairwise comparison: you don't need absolute scores — just relative win rates. Millions of votes collected.
ELO was invented by physicist Arpad Elo for chess. The beauty of pairwise comparison: you don't need a ground truth, just human preference signals at scale.

⚠️ The Benchmark Saturation Problem

  • As models improve, benchmarks become too easy — labs hunt for harder ones; those saturate too
  • Risk: benchmark data leaks into pre-training → scores inflate without real gains
Harder successors: GPQA (PhD-level), ARC-AGI, FrontierMath — but the cycle continues.
GSM8K (Grade School Math, 2021) — 8,500 grade-school word problems. GPT-3 managed only 34%; GPT-4 jumped to 92% in a single generation. By 2024 the benchmark was effectively saturated: further improvement is invisible on this scale. ARC-AGI was deliberately designed by François Chollet to resist saturation through novel visual pattern tasks that require genuine generalisation, not memorisation. It remained robust considerably longer than other benchmarks, though top reasoning models have now approached its upper range too.

Section 4

AI for Scientific Discovery

The biggest impact may be here

🧬 AlphaFold & Protein Structure

  • Proteins fold into 3D shapes — shape determines function; predicting that shape from sequence was a central open problem since Anfinsen's hypothesis (1961)
  • 2018 AlphaFold 1 wins CASP13 — first major AI breakthrough in structure prediction
  • 2020 AlphaFold 2 dominates CASP14 with near-experimental accuracy on single-chain proteins — widely described as solving that specific benchmark problem
  • 2024 AlphaFold 3: extends to protein–DNA, protein–RNA, and small-molecule complexes
200 million+ single-chain structures predicted — previously only ~180,000 known experimentally. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Hassabis & Jumper (DeepMind) for AF2.
Still open: folding dynamics, large complexes, intrinsically disordered proteins.
Precision matters here. The "protein folding problem" has multiple senses: (1) Predicting static 3D structure of a single protein chain from its sequence — this is what CASP measures, and AlphaFold 2 essentially solved it: median GDT_TS ~92 at CASP14, matching experimental resolution. (2) Understanding the physical folding pathway and dynamics — still open; AF2 gives the end-state, not the process. (3) Predicting structures of multi-chain complexes and proteins bound to small molecules — partially addressed by AlphaFold-Multimer and then AlphaFold 3. Anfinsen's thermodynamic hypothesis (1961, Nobel 1972): a protein's 3D structure is uniquely determined by its amino acid sequence. This is what made (1) a well-defined problem. AlphaFold 1 (CASP13, 2018) showed deep learning could compete with physics-based methods. AlphaFold 2 (CASP14, 2020) was qualitatively different — it used attention over multiple sequence alignments and structural frames, and its accuracy leap was so large that CASP organisers described the problem as "largely solved."

💊 Drug Discovery

  • Traditional drug discovery: 10–15 years, $1–2 billion per approved drug
  • AI models can triage billions of molecular candidates in hours using fast surrogate scores — top hits still require accurate re-docking and experimental validation
  • AlphaFold accelerated target identification across the entire industry
AI won't replace clinical trials — it compresses the early-stage search dramatically.
The key bottleneck AI addresses is "virtual screening" — triaging candidates with fast surrogate scores (ML regressors, docking approximations) to filter down billions to thousands. Top hits are then re-docked with higher-accuracy physics-based methods, then synthesised and tested in the lab. AI compresses the early search; clinical trials are unchanged.

🌍 Weather & Climate

  • Traditional forecasting: physics simulations on supercomputers, taking hours
  • Google DeepMind GraphCast (2023): 10-day global forecast in under 1 minute
  • Matched or exceeded ECMWF (world's best physics-based model) on most metrics
  • Climate modelling: AI surrogates can run century-scale scenarios in hours vs. months
ECMWF = European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts — the gold standard for decades.
GraphCast needs physics-based model outputs as input — it's not fully independent. But the speed improvement is real. For climate, running thousands of ensemble simulations becomes accessible with AI surrogates.

The key difference from LLM code generation: these systems reason formally — solutions are provably correct, not just plausible-looking.

⚗️ Materials Discovery & the Synthesis Gap

What AI can do now

  • DeepMind GNoME (Merchant et al., Nature 2023): predicted 2.2 million inorganic crystals that are thermodynamically stable (on the DFT convex hull) — ≈45× the ~48,000 in the Materials Project at the time
  • Of these, 380,000 are the most stable candidates; 736 were retrospectively matched to existing experimental databases — validating the model's accuracy, not new synthesis
  • GNNs predict formation energies in milliseconds vs. hours of DFT simulation

The synthesis gap

  • Thermodynamically stable ≠ synthesisable: a crystal can be the lowest-energy state yet have no known kinetically accessible reaction pathway to make it
  • Precursors, atmosphere, temperature ramp, dwell time — often entirely unknown for predicted structures
  • A-Lab (Szymanski et al., Nature 2023): autonomous robot lab attempted 58 GNoME-predicted targets, successfully synthesised 41 in 17 days — the first closed-loop AI-to-lab pipeline
  • 41 validated vs. 2.2 million predicted: the gap is enormous
The bottleneck has shifted: predicting stable structures is now tractable — knowing how to make them is not. Closing the thermodynamic-prediction-to-physical-synthesis loop is a central open problem in AI for materials science.
GNoME (Graph Networks for Materials Exploration), Merchant et al., Nature November 2023. "Thermodynamically stable" here means the predicted crystal lies on or near the DFT convex hull — i.e., it is the lowest free-energy configuration at 0 K for that composition. This does NOT mean it can be made: a material can be thermodynamically stable yet kinetically inaccessible because there is no feasible reaction path from cheap precursors to that ground state under realistic lab conditions. The 736 figure: GNoME retrospectively checked its 2.2M predictions against existing experimental crystal structure databases (ICSD etc.) and found 736 matches — used as a validation metric showing the model was predicting real structures, not hallucinating. These are not newly synthesised. The 380,000 figure: structures identified as "on the convex hull" (thermodynamically stable without qualification), vs. the broader 2.2M which includes near-hull (metastable) candidates. A-Lab (Szymanski et al., concurrent Nature paper, Nov 2023): an autonomous robotic lab at Berkeley selected 58 target compounds from GNoME predictions, planned synthesis routes using an AI planner, executed solid-state powder reactions robotically, and characterised the results — all without human intervention. 41/58 succeeded. This is the first demonstration of a fully closed AI-to-lab loop for inorganic materials, but the throughput (41 in 17 days) is orders of magnitude below the prediction rate, illustrating the scale of the synthesis gap.

🚀 The Bigger Picture

  • AI may compress decades of scientific progress into years
  • Fields moving fastest: structural biology, materials science, mathematics, climate
  • Human scientists still essential: framing questions, interpreting results, ethics
Risk: AI-generated science that is hard to verify or replicate. The community is still developing norms.
"AI slop science" — papers that use AI to generate plausible-looking results never properly verified. Several journals now flag AI-generated figures. The scientific community is adapting.

The Model Landscape Today

Open weights, closed APIs, and what they mean

What Does "Open Weights" Mean?

  • Closed API: interact via an API — the weights are never released; you use what the provider gives you
  • Open weights: the trained parameters are publicly downloadable — researchers can fine-tune, audit, and run models locally
  • Open weights ≠ open source — training data and code are often still proprietary
  • As of 2026: open-weights frontier models now match or exceed closed models on many benchmarks
The geopolitical story: Chinese labs (Zhipu, DeepSeek, Moonshot, Alibaba) are releasing frontier-class open-weights models under MIT licences. US frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) remain closed.
"Open source AI" is contested. Meta's LLaMA 4 licence restricts commercial use above 700M MAU. True open source would include training code, data, and weights. The open-weights trend is significant: a model that would have been state-of-the-art in 2023 can now run on a single consumer GPU. The gap between open and closed has narrowed dramatically since 2024.

  • Gemma is Google's open-weights family; Gemini remains closed API
LLaMA 4 (Meta, April 2025): Scout (109B/17B active, 16 experts) and Maverick (400B/17B active, 128 experts) are released. Behemoth (~2T/288B active) was announced but never released as of mid-2026. is still training — Meta has shared early benchmark results showing it outperforms GPT-4.5, Claude Sonnet 3.7, and Gemini 2.0 Pro on STEM tasks, but it is not yet publicly released. Its primary purpose is as a teacher model: its outputs are used to distil knowledge into Scout and Maverick via knowledge distillation. All LLaMA 4 models trained on 30T+ tokens of natively multimodal data. Context: 10M tokens on Scout/Maverick — largest available anywhere. GPT-OSS (OpenAI, August 5 2025): Apache 2.0. Two sizes: 120B (117B actual) and 20B (21B actual), both MoE. First open-weight release from OpenAI since GPT-2 (2019) — a major strategic shift driven by competitive pressure from Chinese open-weights releases. GPT-OSS-20B runs on a consumer laptop (16GB RAM). Gemma 4 (Google, April 2 2026): four sizes — E2B, E4B, 26B MoE (3.8B active), 31B dense. Apache 2.0. 256K context, native multimodal, 140+ languages. 31B dense: #3 Chatbot Arena, 89.2% AIME 2026. Gemma is Google's open-weights line; Gemini (2.5 Pro, Flash) remains closed.

The Landscape at a Glance

Open Weights (MIT / similar)

  • Download, run locally, fine-tune freely
  • Auditable — inspect weights and behaviour
  • Community-driven improvement
  • 2026 leaders: DeepSeek-V4, Kimi K2.6, GLM-5.1, Qwen3, LLaMA 4, GPT-OSS, Gemma 4

Closed API

  • Access only via API — weights never released
  • Typically largest estimated parameter counts
  • Provider controls safety, updates, pricing
  • 2026 leaders: GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.7, Grok-4, Gemini 2.5 Pro
The gap is closing: in 2023, closed models led open by a wide margin. By 2026, open-weights models match closed on most benchmarks. The main remaining edge for closed models is at the very top of the scale (GPT-5.5 at ~9.7T estimated) — and that lead is contested.
The convergence of open and closed model performance is one of the most significant developments of 2025–2026. DeepSeek-V4 Pro's 80.6% SWE-Bench Verified score, achieved with MIT-licensed open weights and only 49B active parameters per token, was a major inflection point. The geopolitical framing: Chinese labs are the primary drivers of the open-weights frontier. This creates dual-use tension — the same openness that enables research also enables misuse, and there is no mechanism to enforce export controls on weights once they are downloaded.

Section 6

Governing AI

Safety, innovation, and who decides

🇪🇺 The EU AI Act

  • World's first comprehensive AI law — passed 2024, phased enforcement 2025–2027
  • Risk tiers: Unacceptable (banned) → High → Limited → Minimal
  • Banned: government social scoring, real-time biometric surveillance in public
  • High-risk: hiring AI, medical devices, critical infrastructure — strict requirements
  • Frontier models must document training data and assess systemic risks
Penalties: up to 7% of global annual turnover for the most serious violations.
The risk-tier approach is pragmatic — not all AI is equally dangerous. The "general purpose AI" provisions (added late in negotiations) specifically target foundation models like GPT and Claude.

🇺🇸 The US Approach

  • No comprehensive federal AI law as of mid-2025
  • Biden Executive Order (Oct 2023): safety reporting for powerful AI systems
  • Trump administration (2025): revoked Biden EO — shifted toward "AI dominance"
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework: voluntary guidelines, not law
  • State-level regulation fragmenting (California SB 1047 vetoed 2024)
US approach: sector-by-sector regulation + voluntary commitments from labs.
Critics of the voluntary approach say it's insufficient. The FTC, FDA, and other agencies are developing AI guidance within their existing mandates.

🇨🇳 China's Approach

  • China has regulated specific AI applications since 2022
  • Generative AI Regulations (2023): content must reflect "core socialist values"
  • Watermarking required for all AI-generated content — ahead of most Western jurisdictions
  • Algorithm Recommendation Regulations govern recommendation systems
  • Strong state investment in domestic AI alongside content controls
Model: regulate outputs and content while heavily investing in the underlying technology.
China's approach is "control without restraint on development" — powerful AI, but state-directed content. Domestic champions (Baidu, Alibaba, Zhipu) receive significant state support.

⚖️ Safety vs. Innovation

"Move fast"

  • AI could cure diseases, solve climate change
  • Over-regulating delays these benefits

"Move carefully"

  • We don't fully understand what we're building
  • Mistakes at scale are hard to reverse
Genuinely contested among serious researchers — not just politicians. Both sides have strong arguments.
Geoffrey Hinton (one of the founders of deep learning) resigned from Google in 2023 partly to speak more freely about AI risks. Yoshua Bengio signed the open letter calling for a pause on frontier training. Andrew Ng has been a persistent critic of over-regulation.

⚠️ Near-term vs. Long-term Risks

Near-term (happening now)

  • Deepfakes & election interference
  • Algorithmic bias in hiring/lending
  • Job displacement in specific sectors
  • Academic dishonesty 👀

Long-term (speculative, taken seriously)

  • AI pursuing goals misaligned with human values
  • Power concentration in a few entities
  • Loss of meaningful human oversight
Near-term harms are empirically documented and addressable with policy now. Long-term risks are contested — but a significant minority of researchers who built these systems take them seriously.
Encourage students to engage with the primary debate rather than take anyone's word for it. The near-term harms are real and affect people now. The long-term risks are uncertain but worthy of serious attention.
