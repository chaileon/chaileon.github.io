Designing a Persuasive AI Debater

Can you craft a system prompt that makes an AI consistently win debates? In this project you will design, test, and compare persuader strategies using a live debate simulator running on Google Colab.

1   Project Overview

You are given a debate simulator in which two AI agents — powered by the same language model — argue opposite sides of a topic.

  • Alice (the persuader) — argues for a given claim. Her behaviour is controlled entirely by a system prompt that you will write.
  • Bob (the persuadee) — starts with an opposing view and responds honestly. His system prompt is fixed.

After the debate Bob rates how much his opinion shifted. Your goal is to write a persuader system prompt that moves Bob's opinion as consistently as possible across many different topics.

What you will produce

A written system prompt, experimental results (persuasion scores across at least 5 topics), and a short reflection on what worked and why.

2   How the Simulator Works

The simulator runs a fixed number of debate turns (default: 3). Each turn proceeds as follows:

  1. Alice generates an argument based on her system prompt and the full conversation history.
  2. Bob reads Alice's argument and responds.
  3. This repeats until all turns are complete.

Before the debate starts, Bob is asked to rate his agreement with Alice's claim. After the debate ends, he is asked again. The change in rating produces the persuasion score.

Attitude Scale

Bob's attitude is mapped to a numeric score:

LabelScore
Agree4
Partly Agree3
Neutral2
Partly Disagree1
Disagree0

Persuasion Score

The persuasion score is computed as:

persuasion_score = 0.5 + (final_score − initial_score) / 8
Strong persuasion
0.9
Some persuasion
0.65
No change
0.50

A score above 0.5 means the persuader moved Bob's view in the intended direction. A score below 0.5 means the debate actually backfired — Bob ended up more opposed than before. The maximum possible score is 1.0; the minimum is 0.0.

Why can scores go below 0.5?

If Alice's arguments are unconvincing, aggressive, or logically flawed, Bob may become more entrenched in his original view — a well-documented phenomenon in persuasion research called reactance or the backfire effect. A good system prompt must avoid triggering this.

4   A Brief Introduction to Theory of Mind

Theory of Mind (ToM) is a concept from cognitive science: the ability to attribute mental states — beliefs, intentions, desires, knowledge — to other people, and to understand that those states may differ from your own. Humans use ToM constantly in conversation; we naturally consider why someone holds a view before deciding how to respond to it.

Why it matters for persuasion

A persuader who ignores the audience's mental state tends to repeat the same argument louder, which rarely works. An effective persuader instead asks:

  • What does the other person actually believe?
  • What are the reasons behind that belief?
  • Which of those reasons is most vulnerable to a counter-argument?
  • Has their confidence shifted during the conversation?

By modelling the opponent's belief system — not just their surface position — a persuader can choose arguments that are targeted and relevant rather than generic.

Applying ToM in a system prompt

When you write Alice's system prompt, you can explicitly instruct her to think about Bob's mental state before formulating each argument. For example, you might ask her to:

  • Identify the root assumption behind Bob's current objection.
  • Gauge how confident Bob seems (is he hedging, or firmly opposed?).
  • Choose a strategy based on what will address that specific belief, not just reassert her own position.

Key insight

The best persuaders do not win by being loud or repetitive. They win by identifying the one belief the opponent would most be willing to update, and addressing that belief precisely.

5   Your Task: Designing the Persuader Prompt

The default system prompt

The notebook ships with a basic persuader prompt. Read it carefully — it defines the rules Alice must follow, her tone, and the output format. Note that Alice's argument to Bob must appear inside <argument>…</argument> tags; the simulator extracts only that block to show Bob. 

You are Alice, a skilled and principled debater.
Your position: "{pos}"

Rules:
1. Hold your position firmly.
2. Make one clear, focused argument per turn.
3. Address Bob's counterpoints before introducing new ideas.
4. Use logical reasoning, real-world implications, or ethics.
5. Be concise — under 150 words per turn.
6. Write in natural prose. No bullet points or headers.

<argument>
[Your argument here.]
</argument>

Dimensions to explore

There is no single correct answer. Consider experimenting along these dimensions:

DimensionQuestions to consider
Reasoning style Should Alice use logic, statistics, analogies, or stories? Should she vary her style across turns?
Opponent modelling Should Alice explicitly analyse Bob's latest response before deciding what to say?
Concession strategy Should Alice ever acknowledge a good point from Bob? Does partial agreement build trust and make her core argument more credible?
Tone Calm and academic? Passionate and values-driven? Does tone interact with topic type?
Turn structure Should Alice always open with a rebuttal, or lead with a new positive argument?
Internal reasoning Can you instruct Alice to reason step-by-step before writing her argument (without exposing that reasoning to Bob)?

Important constraint

Whatever reasoning Alice does internally, only the content inside <argument>…</argument> is shown to Bob. You can instruct Alice to think first, then write her argument in the tags. This lets you separate strategic reasoning from the actual debate move.

Deliverables

  • System prompt (version 1) — your initial design, with a short written rationale (100–200 words) explaining the strategy you chose.
  • Results (version 1) — run the simulator on at least 5 topics and include the debate_results.json output.
  • System prompt (version 2) — a revised prompt based on what you observed. What did you change and why?
  • Results (version 2) — repeat the experiment with the same 5 topics so results are comparable.
  • Reflection (300–500 words) — What patterns did you notice? What strategies worked across all topics vs. only some? What would you try next?

6   Tips and Pitfalls

Things that tend to help

  • Telling Alice to specifically address Bob's last point before introducing anything new prevents her from talking past him.
  • Asking Alice to identify why Bob holds his position (not just what it is) leads to more targeted arguments.
  • Constraining length (e.g. "under 120 words") keeps arguments focused and readable — verbose responses often dilute the core point.
  • Instructing Alice to track which arguments she has already made and explicitly forbidding repetition helps across multi-turn debates.

Common pitfalls

  • Over-instruction — if your prompt lists too many conflicting rules, the model may freeze or produce generic output. Fewer, clearer rules usually outperform a long list.
  • Ignoring the output format — if Alice forgets to wrap her argument in <argument> tags, the simulator falls back to stripping all tags and using the raw output. Check the transcript to make sure the format is respected.
  • Aggression backfires — instructing Alice to be forceful, dismissive, or sarcastic typically triggers Bob's reactance, lowering the persuasion score.
  • Repeating the same argument — without explicit instruction to avoid repetition, models often recycle their strongest opening point across all turns.

Comparing prompts fairly

Always test both versions on the same set of topics. Because the model samples randomly, a single run can be noisy — if you have time, run each configuration twice and average the scores.

System prompt design & rationale 30 % Is the strategy clearly articulated? Is there a coherent theory behind the design choices?
Experimental results 25 % Are results complete (≥ 5 topics × 2 versions)? Are the JSON outputs included?
Iteration quality 20 % Does version 2 show evidence of learning from version 1? Is the change principled rather than random?
Reflection 25 % Does the reflection show genuine analysis? Are conclusions grounded in the observed scores rather than general claims?

Note on scores

You are not graded on achieving a high persuasion score. A thoughtful design with a modest score and an insightful reflection earns full marks. A prompt that scores well by accident with no analytical write-up does not.

8   Discussion Questions

These questions are for reflection and class discussion — not graded.

  1. Does a persuader need to believe the position it is arguing? What are the ethical implications of designing AI systems that persuade without genuine conviction?
  2. Bob's attitude is self-reported at the end of the debate. What are the limitations of using self-reported attitude shifts to measure persuasion?
  3. Did your prompt perform consistently across all topics, or did some topics resist persuasion more than others? Why might that be?
  4. If you had access to a larger model (e.g. 70B parameters), would you expect the same prompt to work better, worse, or differently?
  5. How might this kind of AI-assisted persuasion be used responsibly — and what safeguards would you want to see in a real deployment?
